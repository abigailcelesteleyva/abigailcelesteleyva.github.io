Amazon — Investigations Dashboard

Giving investigators their inner Sherlock Holmes back.

Redesigning the Investigations Dashboard inside an existing Case Management System — to help security investigators prioritize, track, and act without friction.

RoleUX Design Intern
TimelineSummer 2024
Type0→1 Feature Design
FocusEnterprise UX, Workflow Design

The setup

An unreleased product just went public.

Imagine a brand-new Amazon Echo — one nobody is supposed to see yet — suddenly shows up on the internet. Engineers have poured months into it. Legal is panicking. Someone needs to figure out exactly how it happened and fast.

Day 0

Photos of an unreleased Amazon Echo appear online. The product hasn't been announced. How did this get out?

12 hours later

A security investigator — let's call her Lisa — gets assigned to find the source of the leak before more information escapes.

Day 1, morning

Lisa opens the Investigations Dashboard. She has 17 active cases. She doesn't know where to start. The tool she relies on most is actively working against her.

That's the problem I was brought in to solve during my summer internship at Amazon.

Discovery

The system was making investigators feel like they were guessing.

I ran surveys and conducted interviews with 18 security investigators to understand their day-to-day reality. What I found was a pattern of anxiety and workaround culture — people adapting themselves to a broken tool instead of the tool adapting to them.

"I feel like I'm guessing what to do next. I just open cases randomly and hope I pick the right one."

The top challenges surfaced across nearly every interview: case prioritization, tracking progress across many cases, and contacting related teams when bottlenecks emerged.

Defining

Two investigators, the same broken tool.

From my research, two distinct profiles emerged with overlapping pain points but different root frustrations.

Lisa
Senior Investigator
Needs to quickly prioritize her caseload and track progress across all of them. Currently digs into each case individually to understand status — a process that can take her entire morning.
"I spend more time figuring out what to work on than actually working."
Raj
Mid-level Investigator
Has been flagged for missing SLA deadlines — not because he's slow, but because he never knew his cases were bottlenecked. By the time he realized, it was too late.
"I got in trouble for something I didn't even know was happening."

Mapping their user journeys revealed three shared moments of failure:

1

Logging in — no starting point

The dashboard shows a wall of case IDs and subjects. Nothing about status, urgency, or what needs attention today. Investigators have to memorize which numbers map to which cases.

2

Case prioritization — forced guessing

With 17 average active cases, there's no system for surfacing what's most critical. Investigators click through cases one by one, hoping to stumble onto something urgent.

3

Tracking progress — invisible bottlenecks

Cases blocked on other teams showed no visual signal. Raj's SLA breaches happened because nothing in the UI communicated that a case had stalled.

The old system

Tables stacked on tables.

Before proposing anything, I audited the existing dashboard. The fundamental problem: every case was represented as a row in a table, with only the case ID and subject visible. Nothing else.

The audit logs were just as unhelpful — timestamped entries with no context. And there was no visual distinction between a P1 case that needed immediate action and a low-priority case from three weeks ago. Everything looked the same.

The core problem

The system was built around data storage, not decision support. It showed investigators everything equally, which meant it helped them with nothing specifically.

Feature 1 — Case prioritization

Give investigators control over their signal-to-noise ratio.

The goal: let investigators pin or star specific cases to get focused updates — reducing the cognitive burden of managing 17 cases while surfacing what actually matters for the day.

I explored three design directions, each with different information density and interaction models.

Design A
Minimal flag + modal
A star icon on each row. Clicking it flags the case; details open in a modal overlay.
✕ Too many clicks to get useful info
Design B — Selected
Inline priority cards
Starred cases surface as rich cards at the top — status, summary, latest update, next step, and lead in one view.
✓ A/B preference + data sufficiency tests
Design C
Full sidebar panel
A persistent right-hand panel showing starred cases. Rich but took up too much real estate.
✕ Reduced space for active case work

Design B was validated through A/B preference tests with 12 investigators and critical data sufficiency tests — confirming that the information displayed was the minimum needed to act without opening the case. Engineers then implemented it; I ran usability tests (time-to-action, information clarity, error rate).

Usability jumped from "Ok" (61) to "Excellent" (88) on the System Usability Scale — a 44% improvement. Time-to-first-action dropped significantly; information clarity errors fell to near zero across test sessions.

Feature 2 — Case transparency

The answer was already in the case log — just invisible.

Investigators needed a way to quickly learn about cases they hadn't opened recently. Three approaches were explored: hover tooltips, inline cards, and pop-up overlays. The question was density vs. disruption.

Option A
Sparse tooltip
Status + subject only. Fast to scan, but left too many questions unanswered.
✕ Not enough info to triage
Option B
Medium tooltip
Added case lead. Better, but investigators still had to open the case for next steps.
✕ Still required a follow-up click
Option C — Selected
Dense tooltip
Status, summary, lead, and next step. Enough to fully triage without opening the case.
✓ Workflow interrupt + backlog triage tests

Key finding

The dense hover provided enough information to triage and dismiss alerts with minimal clicks, confirming its role as a non-disruptive triage tool. Investigators were significantly faster at bottleneck management compared to any pop-up or card solution tested.

Feature 3 — Bottleneck visibility

Investigators didn't need to find the bottleneck. They needed to understand it.

Early in research I assumed the problem was investigators couldn't identify bottlenecked cases. I was wrong. They knew cases were stalled — what they didn't know was where in the process the stall was occurring and who owned it.

I designed a process flow visualization embedded within each case, showing the investigation lifecycle as discrete stages with clear ownership and status signals. Click a stage below to see what an investigator would see.

This component was validated through concept testing and usability testing. The middle-density design — showing the bottlenecked stage with team ownership and days-stalled context — was consistently preferred. Investigators described it as "finally seeing where the fire actually is."

Outcomes

Lisa found her Sherlock Holmes again.

Across all three features, the redesigned dashboard showed substantial improvements in every usability dimension measured. The system went from something investigators actively avoided to a primary tool for starting and managing their day.

Measured results

SUS 61 → 88 — system usability across all features. Time-to-first-action dropped significantly on priority cases. Bottleneck identification dropped from "unknown" to correctly located within the first interaction for 100% of test participants.

What I learned.

User-centered design is only as good as the research behind it. The bottleneck insight — that investigators needed location, not detection — only came from going deeper than surface-level complaints. If I'd stopped at "they can't find bottlenecks," I would have built the wrong thing.

Iteration isn't just changing the design. Each round of testing with investigators produced specific, actionable feedback that changed not just the visuals but the underlying information hierarchy. The SUS jump from 61 to 88 didn't come from polish — it came from repeatedly asking "does this actually reduce your decision-making time?"

Collaboration across functions changes what you build. Working closely with engineers shaped what was feasible; working with investigators shaped what was necessary. Neither alone would have produced the same outcome.
